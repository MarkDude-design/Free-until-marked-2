# Before the Name — workshop performance draft

PUBLIC DEVELOPMENT COPY · based on private editorial v0.6 · 2026-11-01

This fuller draft is outside main's selected reading edition. Personal-history detail and an unconfirmed third-party name have been omitted/generalized for this public copy. The actual shared-making image awaits credit/clearance. Main's spine controls the route; proposed extra passages must earn promotion. Uppercase brackets are silent stage directions. Media references remain planned until the actual inventory is assembled.

## Performance text

### 1. A small card

**[STAGE — HOLD POSTCARD, PICTURE FIRST. LEAVE ROOM TO LOOK.]**

**[ARC — ENCOUNTER FIRST: OFFER AN IMAGE AND ROOM FOR A RESPONSE.]**

I want to begin with something small enough to put in your pocket.

A postcard.

Someone has made a picture, and someone else stops to look.

We can begin there.

It's a connection.

A reaction.

It might be a color that catches you. A memory. A laugh. Something you can't quite name yet.

Or you might put the card down.

That small encounter has room for both of us. I can offer you something, and you can have your own response to it.

A postcard lets the encounter travel. Someone keeps it, sends it, puts it somewhere ordinary, or leaves it behind. It can become part of a day the maker never sees.

**[TURN — THIS ONE CARD → AN IMAGINED GROUP. KEEP “IMAGINE” AUDIBLE.]**

Now imagine a small group making cards together. People choose what they want to make, what to keep, and whether to share it. Some are trying a new tool. Some have been making things for years.

They might approach a museum or gallery with a modest proposal: an agreed space, a small display, a chance for people to meet the makers.

I'm describing something we could build.

The details would matter. So would the pictures.

**[RETURN — THE BROADER PROPOSAL → THIS SMALL ENCOUNTER.]**

But before the whole argument about who gets into the museum, there is this smaller offer: here is something someone made. You have room to respond.

There is a sentence I want underneath that offer:

First off I have zero desire to interfere with your enjoyment of art or to narrate how you do so.

You can decline the postcard.

You can dislike the picture.

That still leaves us somewhere to begin.

**[STAGE — HANG POSTCARD.]**

**[RIFF R1 — RESERVE: ONE CONNECTION OR REACTION TO THE CARD.]**

**[RETURN — “THEN I WANT TO NOTICE HOW MANY DIFFERENT QUESTIONS WE ASK WITH THE SAME FEW WORDS.”]**

**[STOP / SAVE — OPTIONAL FIRST BLOCK ENDS HERE. RESUME AT §2.]**

### 2. The name is doing several jobs

**[STAGE — SHOW NAMING TERMS ONE AT A TIME.]**

A connection, a reaction—that's where I begin looking.

It doesn't give me a complete definition of art. It gives us something to explore.

**[TURN — A SPECIFIC RESPONSE → THE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS OUR LABELS ASK.]**

Then I want to notice how many different questions we ask with the same few words.

“This is a photograph.”

That names a medium.

“This is a portrait.”

That says something about the subject or kind of work.

“This is art.”

That makes a different claim.

“This is good art.”

Well, what do you know? Now we're judging it.

“This is my kind of art.”

Now you know something about me.

**[RIFF R2 — PRIMARY: ONE PERSONAL TASTE EXAMPLE. “FOR ME…” KEEP IT LOCAL.]**

Some of it is amazing because of the story of how it was made. The connections to it can be different.

**[RETURN — “THESE STATEMENTS CAN OVERLAP.”]**

These statements can overlap. They do not have to arrive as a package.

I can recognize something as art and think it is dreadful. I can admire the craft and have no desire to live with the result. Something can matter deeply to its maker and leave me completely cold.

None of those is an especially unusual afternoon with art.

**[TURN — TASTE AND ART-STATUS → PRODUCTION. KEEP THE QUESTIONS DISTINCT.]**

Then we add another label.

“AI-generated.”

Useful information. It tells us something about how an image was produced.

But it isn't a complete answer to every question already on the screen.

It doesn't name every person's contribution. It doesn't supply the history of the source material. It doesn't tell you whether a particular result rewards your attention.

And it certainly doesn't mean we have finished asking whether the production was acceptable.

**[PRECISE — OFFER YOUR NAMING IDEA AS AN INVITATION, WITH ROOM FOR CONTEXT.]**

My starting thought was: before the artist can even have a name, you have to name the art.

I don't mean everything needs a title.

I mean I would like the question of what we are looking at to have a little room before the maker's reputation answers on its behalf.

That is an invitation, rather than a law of art.

Sometimes knowing the maker is part of understanding the work. We will need that part too.

### 3. This may remain rubbish

**[STAGE — SHOW REAL DISCARDED MATERIAL OR ITS HUMAN PHOTOGRAPH.]**

**[TURN — ABSTRACT LABELS → A PARTICULAR OBJECT. LIGHT; LET PEOPLE DISAGREE.]**

This is a piece of discarded material.

I have promoted it to a slide. That's the part I can take responsibility for.

**[LOOK — GIVE THE OBJECT A MOMENT. DESCRIBE WHAT IS ACTUALLY VISIBLE.]**

Maybe the light catches something. Maybe the arrangement interests you. Maybe it is still rubbish, now slightly larger.

You don't have to improve your answer for my benefit.

If I call it found art, the interesting part isn't only the word “art.” It's the series of choices behind “found.”

Someone made the original thing. Someone discarded it. Someone may have photographed it. Someone selected the photograph. I put it here.

Those may be different people.

Calling it found doesn't transfer all their work to me.

And a photograph of rubbish is not the same object as the rubbish.

**[STAGE — SHOW LABELED GENERATED PAPER BESIDE THE REAL MATERIAL.]**

**[PRECISE — REAL MATERIAL → INVENTED IMAGE. KEEP THE SOURCE ACCOUNT TRUE.]**

Here is a generated image of a similar kind of material.

The label matters. I cannot give this a story about where somebody actually dropped it, because that event has not been established by generating the picture.

I can invent a story. Then I need to call it invented.

You may find either image interesting. You may find neither interesting.

That comparison has already opened more than one question: the visible result, the choices in presenting it, and whether the account attached to it is true.

The word “art” won't do all three jobs for us.

**[RIFF R3 — RESERVE: FRAMING OR THE OPTIONAL BANANA OPINION. OWN THE OPINION.]**

**[RETURN — “HERE IS MONET'S WATER LILIES.”]**

### 4. Monet, and a pond

**[STAGE — SHOW CREDITED MONET REPRODUCTION.]**

**[TURN — DISCARDED MATERIAL → A WORK WITH AN ESTABLISHED NAME.]**

Here is Monet's Water Lilies.

**[STAGE — HANG MONET PRINT.]**

We arrive at this with a rather substantial name already attached.

That name can bring knowledge, expectation, affection, boredom, or the memory of being made to write an essay.

The work still has to meet you in some fashion. Recognition isn't the same thing as an encounter.

A reproduction on this screen is also its own situation. It gives us access to an image, but it doesn't put this room inside the painting's original scale and surface.

**[TURN — FAMOUS PAINTING → PARTICULAR HUMAN PHOTOGRAPH.]**

**[STAGE — SHOW HUMAN POND PHOTOGRAPH.]**

Here is a photograph of water.

A different image, made through different decisions. We are not trying to prove it is the same pond.

**[QUIET — LET THE WATER BE SEEN.]**

You can enjoy a pond without joining a practice.

You can look at the reflections. You can remember somewhere. You can decide that this is not how you want to spend your afternoon.

**[STAGE — SHOW THE GENUINE MANUAL GIMP EDIT BESIDE ITS HUMAN SOURCE.]**

**[PRECISE — NAME THE ACTUAL PROCESS. KEEP PHOTOGRAPHER AND ADAPTER DISTINCT.]**

This version is a manual transformation of that photograph. Its actual process belongs in its credit.

An image can acquire a look that you associate with a different process. That association can be a reason to ask for its source. It is not the source itself.

**[STAGE — HANG GENERATED POND IN NORMAL RHYTHM, SOURCE LABEL VISIBLE.]**

There are several images of water here now.

You may prefer one to another. You may find a different reason for each preference.

Those reasons are worth hearing.

A look can invite a question.

We can come back to what the images tell us—and what their sources tell us.

### 5. One picture I will claim

**[TURN — OTHER WORKS → ONE SHARED PERSONAL EXPERIENCE.]**

**[STAGE — SHOW THE REAL SHARED-MAKING PHOTOGRAPH.]**

This is the one picture of my own work that I am going to put forward here as art.

**[PRECISE — SPEAK FOR THAT SHARED MOMENT, NOT FOR EVERY VIEWER.]**

When we looked at it together, we knew it was art.

**[LOOK — LET THE PHOTOGRAPH LEAD.]**

That is how I remember the moment. It wasn't the end of a debate we had won.

We were friends making something together.

I was the artist, shaping an idea. My friend brought her personality to it. Part of what I did was get out of the way so she could express herself.

The silhouette, the howl at the moon—that expression belongs in the account of how the picture happened.

My contribution doesn't disappear when I leave room for hers.

And her contribution doesn't become mine because I'm the one telling you about it.

There is a shared moment in this photograph that I value.

The direction changed while we were making it.

**[RIFF R4 — PRIMARY: ONE REMEMBERED MOMENT OR CREATIVE CHOICE. STAY WITH THE SHARED MAKING.]**

**[RETURN — “YOU DON'T NEED EVERY PART OF MY HISTORY TO RESPOND TO THE PICTURE.”]**

**[STAGE — KEEP HANDS STILL THROUGH THE PERSONAL PASSAGE; HANG THE PHOTOGRAPH AFTERWARD.]**

You don't need every part of my history to respond to the picture.

Knowing some of it may change what you see. That's allowed too.

Some people have told me they recognize it as art even when it isn't their taste. I find that combination useful.

There can be a connection without a desire to own it. Recognition without agreement about its quality. A reaction that takes us somewhere neither of us had planned.

I want to leave room for that.

**[LOOK — LEAVE THE PHOTOGRAPH VISIBLE.]**

**[WIDEN — ONE PERSONAL ART CLAIM → ROOM FOR OTHER PEOPLE'S JUDGMENTS.]**

The other material in this talk can remain photography, found material, things we notice. It doesn't all have to become a claim about my standing as an artist.

This picture gives me a stake in the discussion.

It doesn't give me the deciding vote.

### 6. A credit is useful, and insufficient

**[TURN — PERSONAL TESTIMONY → A SEPARATE FICTIONAL EXERCISE.]**

**[STAGE — SHOW THREE LABELED GENERATED POSTCARD PROPOSALS, EQUAL SIZE.]**

These are three generated proposals for an imaginary postcard.

They are not work made by the participants I imagined at the beginning. We haven't secretly started their project while they weren't here.

This is a separate exercise.

Three proposals for one small space.

**[WIDEN — THREE PARTICULAR IMAGES → THE QUESTION OF SELECTION.]**

The fact that images can be produced doesn't choose which one is worth keeping.

If I choose one, the choice needs more than the sentence “the model made it.”

What am I responding to? What would I lose by choosing another? Does it matter at postcard size? Does an explanation rescue something that the image itself hasn't done?

One person might prefer an alternative for a reason I had overlooked.

And I can do all the work of selecting badly.

Curation is a contribution. It is not a guarantee of quality.

That applies to a folder full of photographs as well as a folder full of generated images.

I could make this talk very easy for myself by finding the worst possible opponent.

Someone who says, “If you liked one of these pictures, you must approve of everything behind it.”

But that would mostly be me arguing with a convenient person I had made up.

**[TURN — AESTHETIC SELECTION → QUESTIONS ABOUT PRODUCTION. LET THE HUMOR FALL AWAY.]**

The harder objection deserves better.

**[QUIET — KEEP THE WORK VISIBLE. GIVE THE OBJECTION ITS FULL WEIGHT.]**

Here is the objection as I understand it.

A result can be interesting while the way it was produced remains objectionable.

There may be questions about what a system used, whose work it depended on, what permission was given, who received credit, who was paid, or who lost work.

The person raising those questions doesn't have to prove the image is ugly before the questions become legitimate.

I agree.

An attractive result does not settle them.

Neither does a clever prompt, a charitable purpose, or a moving story about someone who finally got to make a picture.

Those things can matter. They don't make other people's interests disappear.

**[PRECISE — HOLD BOTH INTERESTS: BEGINNING MAKERS AND PEOPLE AFFECTED BY THE TOOL.]**

And the other direction needs care as well.

We can criticize a particular system or use without making a new maker prove that they deserve to try making anything at all.

That is the tension I want to keep.

People need ways to begin. The people whose work made those ways possible also deserve consideration.

A tool can expand possibilities and still come with serious conditions we have to examine.

The label tells us that generation was involved. A better credit tells us more.

Neither is a certificate that the whole situation is acceptable.

**[CALLBACK — SAME GENERATED POND, BESIDE THE HUMAN PHOTOGRAPH.]**

Let's return to this one.

This pond image was generated. Its label said so when it went up. I didn't stop the conversation to explain it then.

Now that we're looking with that information foregrounded, does anything change for you?

**[LOOK — LEAVE THE QUESTION OPEN.]**

**[RIFF R5 — RESERVE: WHAT CHANGES FOR YOU, IF ANYTHING? NO AUDIENCE TEST.]**

**[RETURN — “THERE ARE FIVE DISTINCT GENERATED VISUAL WORKS IN THIS VERSION OF THE TALK.”]**

**[PRECISE — STATE THE COUNT ONLY WHEN THE FINISHED INVENTORY MATCHES.]**

There are five distinct generated visual works in this version of the talk. The ordinary pond, the paper arrangement, and these three postcard proposals.

They're identified as such. Repeating one doesn't make a sixth.

The manual pond transformation belongs in a different account.

**[TURN — VISUAL SOURCES → WRITING AND EDITORIAL CONTRIBUTIONS.]**

And help with organizing or writing this talk belongs in another account again. There is AI-written draft language in this process; my performed voice and any later changes should not erase that history.

The credits need to tell the truth about the work we actually did.

They do not need to answer every question by themselves.

### 7. Back to the small card

**[CALLBACK — ORIGINAL POSTCARD; RETURN TO THE PROPOSED GROUP.]**

Now we can return to the proposal at the door.

The small group doesn't have to claim it has solved the whole argument.

It can offer something specific.

Here are the people who want to participate. Here is what they want to make. Here are the tools, the proposed terms, the credit, and the parts still open to disagreement.

Here is the space we're asking to use.

**[WIDEN — ONE PROPOSED PROJECT → ROOM FOR STANDARDS, REFUSAL AND PARTICIPATION.]**

A museum or gallery can have standards. It can say this project isn't right for its program. A visitor can dislike the picture without being recruited into a grand moral failure.

And a new maker can still need somewhere to begin.

Those statements are capable of sharing a room.

In the earlier conversation about this, I said:

we find the stuff that we agree on and we expand.

That doesn't mean we expand past somebody's refusal. It means we find an actual useful overlap, rather than congratulating ourselves for having imagined one.

If there are claims about money, access or benefit, a real project would need to show what happened. The good intention is not the result.

And we don't need to turn the makers into a demonstration of our goodness.

They might make something excellent. They might make something awkward, personal, unfinished, or simply not for us.

**[RETURN — GENERAL PRINCIPLE → ONE PRACTICAL POSSIBILITY. “COULD.”]**

Someone buying a card could help the next picture happen.

**[RIFF R6 — RESERVE: WHAT SUPPORT COULD ENABLE NEXT. KEEP IT A PROPOSAL.]**

**[STAGE — OPTIONAL POSTCARD HANDOUT HERE; SETTLE BEFORE THE ENDING.]**

**[RETURN — “THE CARD SHOULD LET US ENCOUNTER WHAT THEY MADE.”]**

The card should let us encounter what they made.

It should also let them remain people whose participation and choices matter after the card has been printed.

That seems a useful place to start.

It leaves a great deal unresolved.

I think the small card can survive that.

### 8. Leave the water some room

**[TURN — DISCUSSION → FINAL ENCOUNTER.]**

**[STAGE — SHOW THE ORIGINAL HUMAN POND PHOTOGRAPH.]**

I began with something small enough to put in your pocket.

This is something you can't put in there.

You can keep a picture of it.

You can change that picture. Make another. Put a name underneath it. Give somebody enough of its history to look again.

You can also stop explaining for a moment.

**[QUIET — LEAVE THE IMAGE. END. REFERENCES WAIT FOR Q&A.]**
