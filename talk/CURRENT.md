### 1. A small card

**[STAGE — HOLD POSTCARD, PICTURE FIRST. LEAVE ROOM TO LOOK.]**

**[ARC — ENCOUNTER FIRST: OFFER AN IMAGE AND ROOM FOR A RESPONSE.]**

I want to begin with something small enough to put in your pocket.

A postcard.

Someone has made a picture, and someone else stops to look.

We can begin there.

It's a connection.

A reaction.

It might be a color that catches you. A memory. A laugh. Something you can't quite name yet.

Or you might put the card down.

That small encounter has room for both of us. I can offer you something, and you can have your own response to it.

A postcard lets the encounter travel. Someone keeps it, sends it, puts it somewhere ordinary, or leaves it behind. It can become part of a day the maker never sees.

**[TURN — THIS ONE CARD → AN IMAGINED GROUP. KEEP “IMAGINE” AUDIBLE.]**

Now imagine a small group making cards together. People choose what they want to make, what to keep, and whether to share it. Some are trying a new tool. Some have been making things for years.

They might approach a museum or gallery with a modest proposal: an agreed space, a small display, a chance for people to meet the makers.

I'm describing something we could build.

The details would matter. So would the pictures.

**[RETURN — THE BROADER PROPOSAL → THIS SMALL ENCOUNTER.]**

But before the whole argument about who gets into the museum, there is this smaller offer: here is something someone made. You have room to respond.

There is a sentence I want underneath that offer:

First off I have zero desire to interfere with your enjoyment of art or to narrate how you do so.

You can decline the postcard.

You can dislike the picture.

That still leaves us somewhere to begin.

**[RIFF R1 — RESERVE: ONE CONNECTION OR REACTION TO THE CARD.]**

**[RETURN — “THEN I WANT TO NOTICE HOW MANY DIFFERENT QUESTIONS WE ASK WITH THE SAME FEW WORDS.”]**

### 2. The name is doing several jobs

**[STAGE — SHOW NAMING TERMS ONE AT A TIME.]**

A connection, a reaction—that's where I begin looking.

It doesn't give me a complete definition of art. It gives us something to explore.

**[TURN — A SPECIFIC RESPONSE → THE DIFFERENT QUESTIONS OUR LABELS ASK.]**

Then I want to notice how many different questions we ask with the same few words.

“This is a photograph.”

That names a medium.

“This is a portrait.”

That says something about the subject or kind of work.

“This is art.”

That makes a different claim.

“This is good art.”

Well, what do you know? Now we're judging it.

“This is my kind of art.”

Now you know something about me.

**[RIFF R2 — PRIMARY: ONE PERSONAL TASTE EXAMPLE. “FOR ME…” KEEP IT LOCAL.]**

Some of it is amazing because of the story of how it was made. The connections to it can be different.

**[RETURN — “THESE STATEMENTS CAN OVERLAP.”]**

These statements can overlap. They do not have to arrive as a package.

I can recognize something as art and think it is dreadful. I can admire the craft and have no desire to live with the result. Something can matter deeply to its maker and leave me completely cold.

None of those is an especially unusual afternoon with art.

**[TURN — TASTE AND ART-STATUS → PRODUCTION. KEEP THE QUESTIONS DISTINCT.]**

Then we add another label.

“AI-generated.”

Useful information. It tells us something about how an image was produced.

But it isn't a complete answer to every question already on the screen.

It doesn't name every person's contribution. It doesn't supply the history of the source material. It doesn't tell you whether a particular result rewards your attention.

And it certainly doesn't mean we have finished asking whether the production was acceptable.

**[PRECISE — OFFER YOUR NAMING IDEA AS AN INVITATION, WITH ROOM FOR CONTEXT.]**

My starting thought was: before the artist can even have a name, you have to name the art.

I don't mean everything needs a title.

I mean I would like the question of what we are looking at to have a little room before the maker's reputation answers on its behalf.

That is an invitation, rather than a law of art.

Sometimes knowing the maker is part of understanding the work. We will need that part too.

### 6. A credit is useful, and insufficient

**[TURN — PERSONAL TESTIMONY → A SEPARATE FICTIONAL EXERCISE.]**

**[STAGE — SHOW THREE LABELED GENERATED POSTCARD PROPOSALS, EQUAL SIZE.]**

These are three generated proposals for an imaginary postcard.

They are not work made by the participants I imagined at the beginning. We haven't secretly started their project while they weren't here.

This is a separate exercise.

Three proposals for one small space.

**[WIDEN — THREE PARTICULAR IMAGES → THE QUESTION OF SELECTION.]**

The fact that images can be produced doesn't choose which one is worth keeping.

If I choose one, the choice needs more than the sentence “the model made it.”

What am I responding to? What would I lose by choosing another? Does it matter at postcard size? Does an explanation rescue something that the image itself hasn't done?

One person might prefer an alternative for a reason I had overlooked.

And I can do all the work of selecting badly.

Curation is a contribution. It is not a guarantee of quality.

That applies to a folder full of photographs as well as a folder full of generated images.

I could make this talk very easy for myself by finding the worst possible opponent.

Someone who says, “If you liked one of these pictures, you must approve of everything behind it.”

But that would mostly be me arguing with a convenient person I had made up.

**[TURN — AESTHETIC SELECTION → QUESTIONS ABOUT PRODUCTION. LET THE HUMOR FALL AWAY.]**

The harder objection deserves better.

**[QUIET — KEEP THE WORK VISIBLE. GIVE THE OBJECTION ITS FULL WEIGHT.]**

Here is the objection as I understand it.

A result can be interesting while the way it was produced remains objectionable.

There may be questions about what a system used, whose work it depended on, what permission was given, who received credit, who was paid, or who lost work.

The person raising those questions doesn't have to prove the image is ugly before the questions become legitimate.

I agree.

An attractive result does not settle them.

Neither does a clever prompt, a charitable purpose, or a moving story about someone who finally got to make a picture.

Those things can matter. They don't make other people's interests disappear.

**[PRECISE — HOLD BOTH INTERESTS: BEGINNING MAKERS AND PEOPLE AFFECTED BY THE TOOL.]**

And the other direction needs care as well.

We can criticize a particular system or use without making a new maker prove that they deserve to try making anything at all.

That is the tension I want to keep.

People need ways to begin. The people whose work made those ways possible also deserve consideration.

A tool can expand possibilities and still come with serious conditions we have to examine.

The label tells us that generation was involved. A better credit tells us more.

Neither is a certificate that the whole situation is acceptable.

**[CALLBACK — SAME GENERATED POND, BESIDE THE HUMAN PHOTOGRAPH.]**

Let's return to this one.

This pond image was generated. Its label said so when it went up. I didn't stop the conversation to explain it then.

Now that we're looking with that information foregrounded, does anything change for you?

**[LOOK — LEAVE THE QUESTION OPEN.]**

**[RIFF R5 — RESERVE: WHAT CHANGES FOR YOU, IF ANYTHING? NO AUDIENCE TEST.]**

**[RETURN — “THERE ARE FIVE DISTINCT GENERATED VISUAL WORKS IN THIS VERSION OF THE TALK.”]**

**[PRECISE — STATE THE COUNT ONLY WHEN THE FINISHED INVENTORY MATCHES.]**

There are five distinct generated visual works in this version of the talk. The ordinary pond, the paper arrangement, and these three postcard proposals.

They're identified as such. Repeating one doesn't make a sixth.

The manual pond transformation belongs in a different account.

**[TURN — VISUAL SOURCES → WRITING AND EDITORIAL CONTRIBUTIONS.]**

And help with organizing or writing this talk belongs in another account again. There is AI-written draft language in this process; my performed voice and any later changes should not erase that history.

The credits need to tell the truth about the work we actually did.

They do not need to answer every question by themselves.
