**Title suggestion:** *Before the Name: A Postcard, an Argument, and an Asterisk*

**One-sentence pitch:** Give the audience a picture and let them decide what it means before telling them how it was made.

## Five-minute spoken cut — original stage draft

*[0:00–0:40 · Hold up a physical postcard or a printed photograph. If there is no print, project the same image. Only use the actual cleared source work.]*

I brought a picture.

It hasn't applied for permission to be art. As far as I know, there's no licensing office for that. If there is, it's probably in a building with a very long queue and a printer that isn't speaking to the network.

You can look at it. You can enjoy it. You can decide it is terrible. You can walk away. The picture doesn't chase you.

*[0:40–1:25 · Let the audience actually look.]*

Now suppose I tell you this image started as a photograph.

Does it change what you're looking at?

Suppose I tell you somebody altered it in GIMP, by hand, and I show you the before and after.

Now you're looking not just at a picture, but at a decision. Somebody chose what stayed, what moved, what disappeared.

Which question did that answer? Is it art? Is it good? Do you like it? Or did it just tell you how it was made?

Those are different questions. We keep stuffing them into one question, and then getting furious because nobody agrees on the answer.

*[1:25–2:25 · Show source and manual edit, if those cleared files are actually available. Otherwise explain them accurately as a described workflow.]*

I make things, I use tools, and I like giving people the room to have a reaction.

Sometimes that's a connection. Sometimes it's a terrible review. Both are better than making the decision for them before they've even looked.

Here's a source. Here's the change. We can disagree about the result and still agree that these two files are not the same event.

That's the part I like about an inspectable workflow. It's not an argument that says, "You have to like this." It's an argument that says, "Here is what happened."

*[2:25–3:35 · Optional generated comparison only if the actual cleared example and its credit sheet are available. If not, use the spoken hypothetical.]*

Now add an AI model.

Somebody types something, makes choices, accepts a rendering, and sometimes does another forty rounds of changes. We can ask what those decisions were.

But "I made choices" is not a universal receipt. It doesn't automatically answer whose work the model saw, whether they consented, whether they were paid, or whether a credit line is missing.

And "it's AI" doesn't answer whether a particular viewer liked the thing. People can disagree about value while asking the same hard questions about production.

There is a beautiful tradition in computing of treating a problem as solved when you have changed the name of the file. I recommend we not do that with art.

*[3:35–4:40 · Return to first postcard.]*

So we can do something very small.

Before the label, offer the image. Give somebody space to react. Then show the source and the process. Keep a record. Give credit where it's owed. Let the viewer revise their judgment—or not.

No one has to agree with your taste. You don't have to give up yours. But an image made with ten different kinds of help shouldn't leave us guessing who did what.

*[4:40–5:00 · Lower the postcard.]*

And if someone asks, "Is it art?"

You can ask a better question first.

"Which part are we talking about?"

That's the beginning of a conversation. The postcard was only the invitation.
